East Asia represents a key region for investigating human adaptive evolution. Numerous studies have identified signals of natural selection in East Asians based on present-day genomes, but population admixture events throughout East Asian may have obscured selection signals, complicating efforts to identify historical selective events and pinpointing the ancestral populations in which these adaptive events occurred. Ancient genomes, which represent the source populations for present-day individuals, can help with investigating positive selection, so we assembled for analysis a large dataset of published ancient genomes from East Asia (N = 900) for which we also performed genomic imputation. Using the Ohana framework, we first performed an unsupervised inference of population structure across ancient East Asia, identifying known components related to ancestries such as Jomon and Tibetan (K=8). We conducted admixture-aware genome-wide selection scans, testing each population individually for loci with allele frequencies deviating from genome-wide covariance expectations. We identified novel candidates under selection and associated historical selective events with particular ancestral lineages. As expected, we detected selection at EPAS1 (P = 8.53) in the ancestry components associated with Qinghai-Tibet Plateau populations, a locus previously implicated in physiological adaptation to low-oxygen environments. We also identified selection on the well-known missense variant rs1229984 in ADH1B (P = 10.32) that influences alcohol metabolism to the component related to southern Chinese and Southeast Asian populations, which supports the connection of this adaptive variant to rice domestication. Finally, we detected a previously unreported selection signal at IFNAR2 (P = 19.85), a gene involved in antiviral immune response, on a component related to Jomon ancestry. Together, our results demonstrate that ancestry-resolved analyses with the aid of ancient genomes can uncover adaptive events that are otherwise difficult to resolve in modern populations, offering new insights into the population context of natural selection in East Asia.
